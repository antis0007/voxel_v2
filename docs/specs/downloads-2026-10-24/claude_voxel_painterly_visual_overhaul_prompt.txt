03. SHARED ARTISTIC LANGUAGE AND DISTINCT SCENE TREATMENTS

Choose a shared world language: expressive detailed voxel forms, broad coherent material masses, selective fine detail, readable light, and atmosphere tied to space. The references differ widely; preserve their useful relationships without forcing one uniform yellow/green grade over every scene.

Common rules:
- Large shapes establish identity before small texture.
- Detail is concentrated around subjects, edges, joints and meaningful ecological transitions.
- Quiet regions are intentional: water, snow, sky openings, fields, wall shafts and shadowed interior masses.
- Foreground, middle distance and background differ in contrast and detail without becoming separate unrelated render styles.
- Vegetation has growth structure, age and ecological placement.
- Water belongs to banks and terrain rather than looking like a colored sheet laid over the world.
- Architecture and plants interact through scale, support, moisture, shade and access.
- Colors vary in related patches and materials, not independently per voxel.
- Voxel edges remain visible and deliberate; detail resolution is applied where it matters.

Maintain several coherent treatments:
Pastoral: pale greens, warm small buildings, broad fields, soft distant mountain layers.
Old-growth: deep canopy, warm/cool light pockets, dark trunks, connected understory, selective flower accents.
Rocky wetland: moss green, gray stone, rusty vegetation, dark reflections, sparse trunks.
Winter: broad off-white snow, cool shadows, dark exposed branches and restrained evergreen accents.
Ruin/courtyard: pale/warm stone, deep openings, green growth and patterned light.
Formal enchanted garden: controlled shapes, ornamental red/gold accents or a green reflective gate, used locally.

Do not confuse painterly with blurry. The close tree, stone stair and foreground ground layer need sharp understandable form. Simplify distant detail and group values while retaining important silhouettes.

Proposed composition:

- Turn the opposite shore into several related sections: a shallow sandy cove, a low darker bank, a small rock/root projection and a sheltered planted margin.
- Group the visible woodland into stands of different age/crown structure, with one stronger old-tree or grove silhouette.

Near-shore detail:

Woodland behind the bank:

06. TERRAIN FORMS, MOUNTAINS AND GEOGRAPHIC COMPOSITION

A06's landscape succeeds because hills and mountains form connected layers. The current visible distant peaks need a stronger relationship than isolated local maxima.

Generation priorities:
1. Plan a small number of dominant ridges, basins and drainage paths.
2. Give ridges direction, varying crest elevation and subordinate spurs.
3. Carve valleys with coherent cross-sections and downstream relationships.
4. Place woodland, fields, scree and rock exposure according to those forms.
5. Add bounded local surface detail after the silhouette and access work.

Do not replace broad landforms with more octaves of noise. More frequency creates a busier version of the same weak shape. Use existing macro terrain systems if available; add only the missing directional controls.

Scale hierarchy:
Dominant mountain or distant hill mass; secondary shoulders/valleys; nearer woodland/hills; local banks and rocks. Avoid making all peaks similarly sized or every ridge equally sharp. In a pastoral scene, softer broad landforms may be more appropriate than dramatic alpine spikes.

Color/coverage follows form:
Forest belts can emphasize slope changes. Exposed rock can reveal ridge structure. Snow, where relevant, gathers on compatible surfaces. Do not paint uniform green over all slopes and then depend entirely on shadows to make geography legible.

Distant geometry:
Use the same planned landforms for far and near representations. Verify shared boundaries, normals/material summaries and edited coverage. A beautiful distant ridge that becomes a different hill on approach fails. Do not hide the mismatch with a stronger fog wall.

Ground-level terrain:
Add modest rises, hollows, root banks, rock shelves and paths that create local views. Keep movement comfortable under the actual player controller. Detailed terrain should not produce constant jumping over ankle-high collision obstacles.

World editing:
All generated terrain remains compatible with excavation/building. Preserve edits and refresh dependent geometry. Art masks must not repaint a player-built floor as grass or respawn rocks in a cleared path.

07. TREE ANATOMY, CROWNS, AGE AND SILHOUETTE

The largest visual return is likely to come from replacing generic visible crown/trunk relationships with a small set of convincing tree families. Verify this through a controlled specimen comparison.

Tree definition:
Species/archetype; age/size class; trunk path and taper; major branch hierarchy; crown envelope; leaf distribution; root/base form; dead/damaged regions; bark/material parameters; interaction identity and bounds.

Start with a restrained useful set:
- Broad old deciduous tree with buttress/base variation and large branching crown.
- Tall straight woodland tree with high canopy and narrow trunk rhythm.
- Irregular conifer with tiered/asymmetric branches.
- Sparse dead/leaf-off tree with readable branching structure.
- Young/suppressed tree and sapling variants.
- One ornamental flowering variant for a localized designed garden.
Do not create dozens of nominal species that share one silhouette.

Trunk construction:
Use a few meaningful bends, gradual taper and strong branch junctions. A mature tree base should connect to terrain with roots, flare or a stable trunk foot. Avoid perfectly straight equal-width sticks for every mature tree. Keep species differences: not every tree needs exaggerated gnarled fantasy roots.

Branch hierarchy:
Build large limbs first, then selected smaller branches. Preserve taper and attachment angles. Do not independently scatter branch cylinders with no growth relationship. Fine twigs are needed selectively: leaf-off silhouettes and hero foreground trees, not every distant crown.

Crown construction:
Define several connected lobes with gaps, varied density and species-specific shape. Large foliage clumps can remain voxelized, but avoid equal cuboids stacked around one axis. Use a broad outer silhouette, smaller irregularities and partial gaps revealing the branch system. Evaluate crown shape in silhouette before surface color.

Leaf/foliage representation:
Choose from existing voxel volumes, compact meshes or instanced clusters according to the engine's supported art/interaction model. Do not enforce expensive physical simulation of every leaf. Do not silently replace the game's solid voxel identity with photographic billboards. Decorative foliage may use cheaper geometry if it preserves the intended appearance and interaction rules.

Age variation:
Young trees have smaller/lower crowns and thinner branches. Mature trees have broader occupied volume. Old trees can lean, split, lose limbs and reveal cavities. Suppressed trees should show limited crowns beneath larger neighbors. Random uniform scale alone cannot express these relationships.

Damage and season:
Remove connected portions or alter growth structure, not random independent leaf voxels everywhere. A dead tree should have a recognizable branching skeleton. Seasonal leaf-off state must reveal enough branches to avoid becoming a bare pole.

Hero tree specimen, A12/A11:
Create one large irregular trunk with major scars, moss/lichen patches, readable roots and an arching limb. Inspect at arm's length, from below, across a clearing and at middle distance. Detail bark near the camera without letting random albedo obscure trunk volume.

Wind:
Use the current animation path or a small coherent extension. Movement should be anchored to plant structure, with trunk/major branches moving less than leaves. Avoid identical phase on every tree or whole solid trunks swaying like grass. Important collision-bearing branches should not visibly move far away from their collision unless the system supports that movement.

LOD:
Retain crown coverage, dominant branch shape and tree height before small leaf detail. Preserve the canopy's shadow mass. A forest must not abruptly become a sparse collection of bare trunks when leaf detail is culled.

08. FOREST STANDS, EDGES, CANOPY AND ECOLOGICAL STRUCTURE

Generate woodland as patches and relationships, not a uniform scatter of independently placed trees.

Stand descriptor:
Dominant family, secondary family, age mixture, density envelope, canopy height range, moisture/soil suitability, clearing pattern, disturbance/damage class and understory profile. Keep this as compact authoring data rather than a full ecosystem simulation.

Placement order:
1. Reserve routes, water edges, building clearances and meaningful open spaces.
2. Place a few structural/hero trees in compatible terrain.
3. Fill canopy stands with subordinate mature trees, respecting spacing and competition cues.
4. Add younger/suppressed trees where light and space support them.
5. Add shrubs/understory patches and fallen wood.
6. Add ground cover and small accents according to local conditions.

Forest edge:
Create a gradual ecological transition where appropriate: meadow, grass/flower patches, shrubs/saplings, exposed larger trunks, connected canopy. Some edges can be abrupt because of water, rock or clearing; make that boundary legible. Avoid a straight line of identical trees along a biome edge.

Forest interior:
Use trunk spacing, canopy openings and understory suppression to form navigable rooms. Dense canopy does not require dense ankle-high obstacles everywhere. Include pockets of shade, sunlit clearings, fallen-log crossings and quiet ground.

A07 grove/island:
Build one compact stand whose crowns read as a coherent elevated mass. Place tall trunks and a sparse bare tree along selected edges. Give the island/shore sufficient width and real interior space. Preserve the reflection silhouette without copying the painted mass into an impossible opaque wall of leaves.

Ecological relationships:
Roots grip banks and rocks; moss collects in suitable places; fallen trees alter local vegetation; canopy shade changes ground cover. A small number of these relationships makes a scene much richer than independent random assets.

Spacing/determinism:
